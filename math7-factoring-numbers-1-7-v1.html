Key idea

A prime number has exactly two factors. A composite number has more than two factors. Every composite number can be written in only one way as a product of primes: its prime factorization. You can find it with a factor tree or with a step diagram.

This page explains each kind of question once. Exercises that repeat the same question are explained together, because the method is the same.

1. Name the term Vocabulary 1–3

The question: “Which term means: a natural number that has exactly two factors, 1 and itself?”

How to solve it:

  1. Read the sentence in the text on page 32.
  2. Match it with one of the three yellow Vocabulary words: prime number, composite number, prime factorization.
  3. Type the term.
Worked example. “A natural number that has more than two factors” → composite number.

The core idea: prime = exactly two factors; composite = more than two; prime factorization = the product of prime factors.

2. Prime factorization with a factor tree Example 1; exercises 1–8, 17–28, 41–44

The question: “Write the prime factorization of 36.”

How to solve it:

  1. Write the number at the top and split it into two factors (any two work).
  2. If a factor is not prime, split it again. Continue until every factor at the end of a branch is prime.
  3. Write all the prime leaves with × between them.
  4. If a prime repeats, use an exponent: 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 2² × 3².
Worked example (book). 36 = 4 × 9; 4 = 2 × 2 and 9 = 3 × 3; so 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 2² × 3².
Exercise 18. 75 = 5 × 15; 15 = 3 × 5; so 75 = 3 × 5 × 5 = 3 × 5².

The core idea: a prime cannot be split any more, so you stop there. Any first split gives the same primes.

3. Prime factorization with a step diagram Example 2; exercises 9–16, 29–40

The question: “Write the prime factorization of 252.”

How to solve it:

  1. Divide the number by the smallest prime that divides it. Write the quotient below.
  2. Keep dividing the new number by the smallest possible prime.
  3. Stop when the quotient is 1.
  4. The primes you divided by are the prime factors. Write them with × and use exponents for repeats.
Worked example (book). 252 ÷ 2 = 126, ÷ 2 = 63, ÷ 3 = 21, ÷ 3 = 7, ÷ 7 = 1. So 252 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 7 = 2² × 3² × 7.
Exercise 10. 90 ÷ 2 = 45, ÷ 3 = 15, ÷ 3 = 5, ÷ 5 = 1. So 90 = 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = 2 × 3² × 5.

The core idea: check with a quick multiplication: the primes must multiply back to the number. The book’s note for Example 2(b) prints 459 by mistake; the number is 495.

4. How many prime factorizations? Other factor pairs Exercises 45, 46

The question: “One way to factor 64 is 1 × 64. What other ways can 64 be written as the product of two factors?” / “How many prime factorizations of 64 are there?”

How to solve it:

  1. For pairs: find the numbers that divide 64 exactly and write each with its partner.
  2. For “how many”: use the rule on page 33: a composite number has only one prime factorization.
Exercise 45. 64 = 2 × 32 = 4 × 16 = 8 × 8.
Exercise 46. 64 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 2⁶, and there is only one prime factorization.

The core idea: every starting pair leads to the same prime factors.

5. Write the composite number from its prime factorization Exercises 47–54

The question: “Write the composite number for 2² × 3² × 5.”

How to solve it:

  1. Work out each power: 2² = 4 and 3² = 9.
  2. Multiply all the numbers: 4 × 9 × 5.
  3. Multiply two at a time: 4 × 9 = 36, then 36 × 5 = 180.
Exercise 48. 2⁶ × 5 = 64 × 5 = 320.

The core idea: this is the reverse of factoring: multiply the primes back together.

6. Number puzzles with prime factors Exercises 55, 56, 60

The question: “If the prime factors of a number are all the prime numbers less than 10 and no factor is repeated, what is the number?”

How to solve it:

  1. Turn the words into a list of primes (less than 10: 2, 3, 5, 7).
  2. Multiply them, or compare the prime factorizations of the numbers in the question.
  3. Check by factoring your answer again.
Exercise 55. 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 = 210.
Exercise 56. 28 = 2 × 2 × 7 and 63 = 3 × 3 × 7: the shared prime is 7.
Challenge 60. Use three 2s (from 8), two 3s (from 9), one 5 and one 7: 8 × 9 × 5 × 7 = 2 520.

The core idea: prime factorizations show what a number is made of.

7. Farm diagram Exercise 57

The question: “Side lengths are factors of 308 and one side is prime. Which areas in the diagram have the correct dimensions?”

How to solve it:

  1. Write the prime factorization of 308: 2 × 2 × 7 × 11.
  2. For each area check: does each side divide 308? Is one side prime?
  3. Name the areas that pass both tests.
Answer. Chicken coop 7 × 44 (7 is prime) and Sheep pen 11 × 28 (11 is prime). Barn 19 × 22: 19 does not divide 308. Pig pen 14 × 22 and Garden 4 × 77: no side is prime.

The core idea: two tests for every area: factor of 308, and one prime side.

8. Write a problem and write about it Exercises 58, 59

The question: “Write a problem using prime factorization that includes the calories per serving of the melons.” / “Describe how to use factor trees …”

How to solve it:

  1. Choose a number from the table (66, 15 or 42) and ask for its prime factorization.
  2. To describe a factor tree: say what you write first, how you split, and when you stop.
Example problem. “One serving of cantaloupe has 66 calories. Write the prime factorization of 66.” Answer: 66 = 2 × 3 × 11.Mine, not from the book

The core idea: use the numbers from the page and show the factorization.

9. Round a square root Exercises 61–64 (Lesson 1-6)

The question: “Round √134 to the nearest integer.”

How to solve it:

  1. Find the perfect squares on each side: 11² = 121 and 12² = 144.
  2. See which one 134 is closer to: 134 − 121 = 13 and 144 − 134 = 10.
  3. It is closer to 144, so √134 rounds to 12.
Exercise 62. √11 is between 3 (9) and 4 (16); 11 is closer to 9, so the answer is 3.

The core idea: a square root lies between two whole numbers.

10. Order of operations Exercises 65–67 (Lesson 1-5)

The question: “Find the value of 14 − 3 × 2 + 4.”

How to solve it:

  1. Brackets first.
  2. Then exponents.
  3. Then multiply or divide, left to right.
  4. Then add or subtract, left to right.
Exercise 66. (3 + 2)² ÷ 5 − 5 = 5² ÷ 5 − 5 = 25 ÷ 5 − 5 = 5 − 5 = 0.

The core idea: the order of operations gives the same answer for everyone.

11. Test prep with powers Exercises 68, 69 (Lesson 1-2)

The question: “What is the value of 4⁶?” / “Find the value of 2³ + 3².”

How to solve it:

  1. An exponent tells how many times the base is multiplied by itself (4⁶ is not 4 × 6).
  2. Work out each power, then add.
  3. Match your result with the choices.
Exercise 69. 2³ = 8 and 3² = 9, so 8 + 9 = 17: choice c.

The core idea: check every choice before you answer.

12. Think and Discuss Think and Discuss 1–3

The question: “Explain how to decide whether 47 is prime.” / “Compare prime numbers and composite numbers.” / “Tell how you know when you have found the prime factorization.”

How to solve it:

  1. Use the definitions on page 32 (prime: exactly two factors; composite: more than two).
  2. For 47: check the small primes 2, 3, 5.
  3. You are finished when every factor is prime (tree) or the quotient is 1 (step diagram).
Example for 47. 47 is odd, 4 + 7 = 11 is not a multiple of 3, it does not end in 0 or 5, and 7 × 7 = 49 is already more than 47: no number except 1 and 47 divides it, so 47 is prime.Mine, not from the book

The core idea: use your own words; many answers are correct.
