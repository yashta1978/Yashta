Key idea

To multiply by a power of ten, move the decimal point to the right; to divide, move it to the left. The number of zeros (or the exponent) is the number of places. To convert to a smaller unit multiply; to a larger unit divide.

This page explains each kind of question once. Exercises that repeat the same question are explained together, because the method is the same.

1. Multiply by a power of ten Example 1(a); exercises 1, 3, 12, 13, 15, 28, 30

The question: “Multiply: 4 325 × 1 000.” / “6.0912 × 10⁵.”

How to solve it:

  1. Count the zeros in the power of ten, or read its exponent: 1 000 has 3 zeros; 10⁵ has the exponent 5. That number is how many places to move the decimal point.
  2. To multiply, move the decimal point that many places to the right.
  3. If there are not enough digits, write placeholder zeros.
Worked example (book). 4 325 × 1 000: there are 3 zeros in 1 000, so move the decimal point 3 places right: 4 325.000 = 4 325 000.
Exercise 3. 6.0912 × 10⁵: move the decimal point 5 places right (one placeholder zero): 609 120.

The core idea: multiplying by a power of ten makes the number larger: the decimal point moves right.

2. Divide by a power of ten Example 1(b), 1(c); exercises 2, 14, 16, 17, 29

The question: “Divide: 4 325 ÷ 1 000.” / “79.95 ÷ 10⁴.”

How to solve it:

  1. Count the zeros or read the exponent: that is the number of places.
  2. To divide, move the decimal point that many places to the left.
  3. Write placeholder zeros in front when the digits run out.
Worked example (book). 79.95 ÷ 10⁴: the power of 10 is 4; move the decimal point 4 places left and write placeholder zeros: 0.007 995.
Exercise 17. 42 516 ÷ 10 000: 4 zeros, so move the decimal point 4 places left: 4.2516.

The core idea: dividing by a power of ten makes the number smaller: the decimal point moves left.

3. Expressions with powers of ten Exercises 31, 32

The question: “Find the value of 11.6 − 42 ÷ 10².” / “(2.3 + 5.67) × 10⁵.”

How to solve it:

  1. Use the order of operations: brackets first, then multiply or divide, then add or subtract.
  2. Do the division or multiplication by the power of ten with the decimal-point rule.
  3. Then add or subtract: line up the decimal points.
Exercise 31. 42 ÷ 10² = 0.42; then 11.6 − 0.42 = 11.18.
Exercise 32. brackets: 2.3 + 5.67 = 7.97; then 7.97 × 10⁵ = 797 000.

The core idea: finish the brackets and the division before you subtract.

4. Prefixes (Reading Math) Vocabulary 1; page 12

The question: “What do kilo, centi and milli mean?”

How to solve it:

  1. Read the Reading Math box on page 12.
  2. Kilo means thousand, centi means hundredth, milli means thousandth.
  3. A kilometer is a thousand meters; a centimeter is a hundredth of a meter; a millimeter is a thousandth of a meter.
Worked example (book). Kilo: thousand. Centi: hundredth. Milli: thousandth.

The core idea: a prefix tells you how a unit compares with the base unit.

5. Choose the most appropriate metric unit Example 2; exercises 4–7, 18–22

The question: “A pencil is about 15 ? long.”

How to solve it:

  1. Find a thing of about the same size in the table on page 12 (“Approximate Comparison”).
  2. Think: how many of those does the object equal?
  3. Use the abbreviation of that unit: cm, m, km for length; g, kg, ton for mass; mL, L for capacity.
  4. Check that the number makes sense (15 m would be much too long for a pencil).
Worked example (book). A pencil is about 15 little-finger widths: 15 cm. A man has a mass of about 75 textbooks: 75 kg. A gallon holds about 4 bottles of water: 4 L.
Exercise 20. A box of cereal is about half a kilogram: 540 g.

The core idea: compare with a familiar object, then check the size.

6. Convert a measure within the metric system Example 3; exercises 8–11, 23–27, 33–36

The question: “Convert: 6 500 g = ? kg.” / “0.25 L = ? mL.”

How to solve it:

  1. Decide whether the new unit is smaller or larger.
  2. Smaller unit: multiply by a power of ten. Larger unit: divide by a power of ten.
  3. Find the power of ten from the relation (1 kg = 1 000 g, 1 L = 1 000 mL, 1 m = 100 cm, 1 ton = 1 000 kg).
  4. Move the decimal point that many places: right to multiply, left to divide.
Worked example (book). 6 500 g = (6 500 ÷ 1 000) kg = 6.5 kg. 0.25 L = (0.25 × 1 000) mL = 250 mL.
Exercise 36. 17.89 m = 17.89 × 1 000 mm = 17 890 mm.

The core idea: to a smaller unit: multiply; to a larger unit: divide.

7. Compare measures in different units Exercise 37

The question: “Kawa is 91.4 cm tall. Serdar is 1.83 m tall. Who is taller? How much taller?”

How to solve it:

  1. Write both measures in the same unit: 1.83 m = 183 cm.
  2. Compare the numbers: 183 is greater than 91.4.
  3. Subtract to find the difference: 183 − 91.4 = 91.6.
Exercise 37. Serdar is taller, by 91.6 cm.

The core idea: never compare numbers that have different units.

8. Explain in words Think and Discuss 1–2; exercise 38

The question: “When you multiply or divide by a power of ten, how do you know how many places and which direction to move the decimal point?”

How to solve it:

  1. Say what tells you the number of places: the number of zeros or the exponent of the power of ten.
  2. Say what tells you the direction: multiply = right, divide = left.
  3. Add a short example from the page.
Worked example (book). Page 12: the number of zeros, or the exponent, tells how many places. Page 13 Helpful Hint: to convert to smaller units, multiply; to larger units, divide.

The core idea: use your own words; many answers are correct.

9. Challenge: use a given fact Exercise 39

The question: “About 3.281 feet equal 1 meter. About how many feet are equal to 2.5 meters?”

How to solve it:

  1. Read the fact: 1 meter ≈ 3.281 feet.
  2. 2.5 meters is 2.5 times as long, so multiply: 3.281 × 2.5.
  3. Round the answer sensibly (the question says “about”).
Exercise 39. 3.281 × 2.5 = 8.2025, so about 8.2 feet.

The core idea: multiply the given fact by the number of meters.

10. Spiral review (earlier lessons) Exercises 40–47

The question: “Evaluate 7 × 33.” / “Evaluate 8².” / “Which expression does not equal 7 × (34 + 23)?”

How to solve it:

  1. Adding and multiplying: use easy pairs (4 × 5 = 20) or break a number into parts (33 = 30 + 3).
  2. Powers (Lesson 1-2): use the base as a factor as many times as the exponent says; ¹ keeps the number, ⁰ gives 1.
  3. Test Prep: work out the value of the given expression first, then work out each choice.
Exercise 42. 7 × 33 = 7 × 30 + 7 × 3 = 210 + 21 = 231.
Exercise 47. 7 × (34 + 23) = 399; choice c: 7 × 34 + 23 = 261, so c is not equal.

The core idea: powers: exponent counts the factors; brackets first.
